How to write a scholarship essay that gets shortlisted

A clear structure, real examples of what works and a revision checklist for statements of purpose and scholarship essays.

By the iwin.in editorial team. Updated 16 October 2026.

Selection panels read hundreds of essays. The ones that get shortlisted aren't the most impressive on paper; they're the ones that make the reader understand, quickly and specifically, who the applicant is and what the money will change. Here's a structure that works for most scholarship and fellowship prompts.

Answer the question they asked

Before writing, underline every part of the prompt. "Describe a challenge you overcame and how this scholarship will help you achieve your goals" has three parts: a challenge, how you overcame it, and how the scholarship links to your goals. Many essays answer only the first. Make a short plan with one paragraph or more for each part.

A structure that works

  1. A specific opening scene (2–4 sentences). Start with a moment, not a quotation or a definition. "The power cut at 7 p.m. every night meant I solved physics problems by the light of my father's phone" tells the reader more than "I have always been passionate about science".
  2. What you did (the core). Show actions and results. Numbers help: "I started a weekend maths circle for 14 younger students in my village; nine passed the board exam with first division."
  3. What you learned. One honest insight, linked to how you work now.
  4. Where you're going. A concrete goal for the next two to five years, and why this programme or course is the right step.
  5. Why this scholarship. Name what it will change: fees you couldn't pay, a course you couldn't access, time freed from part-time work. Be direct; panels appreciate clarity about need.

Show, don't list

Your CV already lists achievements. The essay should explain the one or two that matter most, in depth. Choose experiences that show the qualities the scholarship values. A leadership award wants evidence of leading; a research fellowship wants curiosity and method.

Writing in your own voice

Write the way you'd explain your story to a respected teacher: clear, honest and specific. Avoid inflated vocabulary. If English isn't your strongest language, short sentences are your friend. Clarity beats decoration every time.

If you use AI tools for brainstorming or proofreading, make sure the experiences, examples and wording remain yours. Many programmes now ask applicants to confirm their essays are their own work, and generic text stands out to experienced readers.

Revision checklist

  • Does the first paragraph contain a specific person, place or moment?
  • Does every paragraph answer part of the prompt?
  • Have you replaced vague claims ("hard-working", "passionate") with evidence?
  • Is your goal concrete enough that a reader could picture it?
  • Have you stayed within the word limit? Going over can lead to disqualification.
  • Has someone else read it aloud to you? Hearing it exposes clumsy sentences.
